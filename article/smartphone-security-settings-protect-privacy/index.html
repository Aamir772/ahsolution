8 Smartphone Security Settings to Protect Your Privacy

Introduction

Your phone may contain private messages, financial apps, photos, work accounts and access to your email. If someone gains access to it—or to an account connected to it—the impact can extend well beyond the device itself.

The good news is that a few settings can reduce common risks. The steps below apply broadly to Android and iPhone, although menu names and available features differ.

1. Use a strong screen lock

Choose a strong PIN or password. Enable fingerprint or face authentication if your device supports it and you are comfortable using it.

Avoid short, predictable PINs. Do not share your device passcode casually, and remember that biometric unlocking is usually backed by a passcode or password.

2. Turn on multi-factor authentication

Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA) for your primary email, financial accounts and important social accounts. MFA requires an additional proof of identity beyond the password.

Where supported, use a passkey or a phishing-resistant security key. An authenticator app can also be useful. SMS codes are generally better than having no additional factor, but they can be vulnerable to number takeover and social engineering.

3. Review camera, microphone and location permissions

Check which apps can access your camera, microphone, contacts, photos and location. Revoke permissions that are unnecessary for an app's core purpose.

On Android, look for Permission manager or Privacy Dashboard. On iPhone, open Settings → Privacy & Security and review the permission categories.

Some apps need location only while in use; others may not need it at all.

4. Install operating-system and app updates

Updates can include fixes for security weaknesses. Enable automatic updates where practical and install updates from the operating system's official update mechanism or the official app store.

If your phone no longer receives security updates, review the manufacturer's support policy and consider whether it is time to move to a supported device.

5. Enable device-finding and theft protections

Configure the built-in device-finding service before you lose your phone. Check that you can access the relevant account from another device.

Enable available theft-protection features, and understand how to remotely locate, lock or erase the phone if needed. Availability varies by operating system, model and region.

Never share your account password or verification codes with someone claiming they can recover your phone.

6. Hide sensitive lock-screen notifications

Message previews and one-time codes can expose private information when the device is locked.

Choose an option that hides sensitive notification content until the phone is unlocked. You can often keep alerts visible without displaying the full message.

7. Use safer browsing and messaging habits

Enable your browser's available safe-browsing protections and use spam filtering where supported. Treat urgent requests for money, passwords or verification codes as suspicious.

Do not install an app from an unexpected link merely because a message claims it is required to secure your account.

8. Back up important data and secure account recovery

Check that important photos, contacts and documents are backed up. Make sure you know which account stores the backup and that you can recover that account.

Secure the recovery email address and phone number associated with your main accounts. A backup that you cannot access during an emergency is of limited use.

A quick privacy audit

Set aside ten minutes every month to:

  • Review apps you no longer use and uninstall them.
  • Recheck sensitive permissions.
  • Review account sign-in activity.
  • Update your operating system and apps.
  • Confirm that backups are current.
  • Check that device-finding features work.
  • Remove unfamiliar devices from important accounts.

What not to do

Do not reuse one password across multiple accounts.

Do not share one-time passwords or authentication codes.

Do not assume a caller is genuine because the displayed number looks familiar.

Do not install security apps from random links or pop-ups.

Do not assume a VPN makes every online activity private or safe.

Do not rely on a single security feature to stop every attack.

Frequently asked questions

Is a fingerprint lock enough to secure a phone?

It is useful, but it is only one layer. Keep a strong device passcode, install updates and protect the accounts linked to your phone.

Do I need a VPN?

A VPN can protect certain network traffic between your device and the VPN provider, but it does not automatically prevent phishing, malicious apps or account compromise. Choose one based on a specific need and review its privacy policy.

What should I do if I think my phone or account is compromised?

From a trusted device, secure your primary email and important accounts, review active sessions, revoke unfamiliar access and contact your bank if financial information may be affected. Use the device manufacturer's official recovery guidance if you suspect the phone itself is compromised.

Conclusion

Good smartphone security is a routine, not a single app. Start with a strong lock, MFA, careful permissions and regular updates. Then make sure your recovery options and backups work before you need them.
