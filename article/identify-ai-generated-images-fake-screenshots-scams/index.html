How to Identify AI-Generated Images, Fake Screenshots and Online Scams

Introduction

AI-generated images, edited screenshots and convincing scam messages are becoming easier to create. A picture can look realistic without showing a real event, and a screenshot can be altered to make a false claim appear genuine.

You cannot reliably identify every AI-generated image just by looking at it. The safest approach is to combine visual inspection with source verification, reverse-image search and checks against trustworthy evidence.

This guide explains a practical process anyone can use before sharing an image or acting on a suspicious message.

1. Inspect the image—but do not rely on visual clues alone

Look for details that seem inconsistent with the scene:

  • Text that is misspelled, distorted or inconsistent.
  • Reflections, shadows or lighting that do not match.
  • Repeated patterns or objects that appear unnaturally similar.
  • Jewellery, fingers, edges or background details that seem malformed.
  • Objects that appear to merge into one another.
  • Different parts of the image that seem to follow different perspectives.

These clues can be useful, but they are not proof. AI image generation is improving, and ordinary photographs can also contain visual distortions caused by compression, motion or editing.

Rule: visual inspection should raise questions, not settle the case.

2. Find the original source

Ask where the image first appeared. Is it from an official organisation, a named photographer, a reputable news outlet or an anonymous account?

Search for distinctive phrases from the caption. If the image claims to show a breaking event, look for independent reporting from credible sources.

Be cautious when multiple accounts repeat the same image but all trace back to one unverified post. Repetition does not equal independent confirmation.

3. Use reverse-image search

A reverse-image search can help locate earlier appearances of an image, similar versions or a different context in which it was originally published.

Try Google Lens or TinEye. Upload the image or use the service's available image-search method, then inspect the results.

Look for:

  • An earlier publication date.
  • A different caption attached to the same image.
  • Cropped or edited versions.
  • An original image that contradicts the viral claim.
  • A credible source explaining the image's context.

A lack of results does not prove that an image is AI-generated. New, private or heavily modified images may not appear in search results.

4. Check for AI labels and provenance information

Some AI-generated media includes labels, metadata or provenance credentials that indicate how it was created or edited. These signals can be useful when available.

For example, Google's SynthID uses imperceptible digital watermarks in supported AI-generated content. Content Credentials provides information about content provenance where supported.

There are important limitations:

  • Not all AI-generated media carries a detectable watermark.
  • Metadata can be removed when files are edited or shared.
  • Provenance information may be missing or incomplete.
  • A credential can help describe a file's history, but it does not automatically prove that every claim about the scene is true.

Treat provenance tools as one part of verification, not a universal AI detector.

5. Verify screenshots independently

A screenshot of a payment, chat, news headline or official notice is not enough to prove that the event happened.

For a suspicious screenshot:

  • Search for the quoted text on the original website or platform.
  • Open the original page or message if you have legitimate access.
  • Check the date, account identity and surrounding context.
  • Look for the claim on the organisation's official website.
  • Confirm financial transactions inside your own banking or payment app.
  • Contact the organisation through a verified contact channel if necessary.

Do not trust a payment receipt sent by another person as proof that funds have reached your account. Check your own transaction history.

6. Recognise the warning signs of an online scam

Scammers often try to rush you into acting before you can verify a claim. Watch for messages that:

  • Demand immediate payment or threaten account closure.
  • Ask for passwords, PINs or one-time verification codes.
  • Send unexpected links to sign in or update account details.
  • Offer prizes, jobs or returns that seem too good to be true.
  • Ask you to install remote-access software without a legitimate reason.
  • Request payment through unusual or difficult-to-reverse methods.
  • Claim to be a relative, manager, bank employee or government official but resist independent verification.

AI can make scam messages more fluent and can help fraudsters imitate voices or identities. A message that sounds professional—or a voice that sounds familiar—should still be verified when money or sensitive information is involved.

7. Verify urgent requests through another channel

If someone claims to be a family member, colleague or bank representative and requests money, pause.

Call a known number from your contacts or the organisation's official website. Do not rely only on a phone number, email address or link included in the suspicious message.

For family or workplace emergencies, a pre-agreed verification phrase or callback procedure can help. Never disclose authentication codes to someone who contacts you unexpectedly.

8. Report suspicious content and limit its spread

If you suspect an image or message is fraudulent:

  • Avoid forwarding it as fact.
  • Save relevant evidence if it is safe and lawful to do so.
  • Report the post or account through the platform's reporting tools.
  • Contact the affected organisation through its official channel.
  • If you have lost money or shared financial credentials, contact your bank promptly.

In India, cybercrime complaints can be filed through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal. If money has been lost to cyber fraud, contact your bank immediately and use the official cybercrime reporting channels. The national cybercrime helpline is 1930; act promptly and verify current guidance on official government channels.

A simple verification checklist

Before you share a surprising image or act on a suspicious message, ask:

  • Who originally published it?
  • Can I find the same image in an earlier context?
  • Do independent, trustworthy sources confirm the claim?
  • Is there provenance information—and what does it actually establish?
  • Can I verify the event through an official source?
  • Is someone pressuring me to act, pay or reveal information immediately?

If the claim cannot be verified, do not present it as confirmed.

Frequently asked questions

Can an AI detector tell me for certain whether an image is fake?

No detector is universally reliable across all images, generators, editing methods and compression levels. Use detection tools cautiously and combine them with source and context checks.

Does a realistic image prove that an event happened?

No. A realistic image can be generated, staged, edited or shown with a misleading caption. Verify the event independently.

Can metadata prove an image is genuine?

Metadata and provenance records can provide useful clues, but they may be missing, altered or incomplete. They do not independently prove every detail depicted in an image.

What should I do if I have already paid a scammer?

Contact your bank or payment provider immediately, report the incident through official channels and preserve relevant messages and transaction records. Do not pay anyone who promises guaranteed recovery of your money.

Conclusion

You do not need to become a forensic expert to avoid many digital scams. Pause when a claim creates urgency, trace the image to its source, verify screenshots independently and confirm payment or account activity through official channels.

In a world where convincing media can be created quickly, verification matters more than appearance.
